Family Game Shelf · new game idea

The Runaway Egg

A choose-your-own dinosaur adventure. Each page is one illustrated scene, a few lines of story and 2–3 choices. The characters answer whatever you pick, and the story changes as you go. Below: four story ideas, the full plan for my favourite, and six art styles to choose from.

Four story ideas

Each one fits 7–20 scenes per play and has room for real branching. I'd build the first one, but any of them works with the same engine and art.

My pick

The Runaway Egg

Cozy adventure · ages 4–9

A rumble shakes Fern Valley and a big speckled egg rolls right up to Pip, a young Parasaurolophus. Pip sets off to Smoke Mountain to find where it belongs. The twist at the end: the rumbling was never the volcano. It was a very worried T. rex mom stomping around looking for her egg.

Why it works: the goal is instantly clear (take the baby home), the egg wiggles more each scene so time feels like it's passing, the T. rex is scary-then-sweet, and the hatching is a big payoff.

The Volcano Wakes Up

Hero journey · a little more exciting

Smoke Mountain starts puffing. Pip has to warn the herds and lead everyone to Crystal Lake. Choices are about who to help and which way to go, and the ending changes with who made it there with you.

Good if you want more stakes and a "rescue everyone" feeling. Needs care so it stays fun rather than scary.

Who Ate the Moonberries?

Mystery · ages 6–10

The valley's Moonberry bush has been picked clean overnight. You question suspects (sneaky raptors, a hungry Ankylosaurus, a sleepy Brachiosaurus), collect clues and pick who you think did it. The culprit is a surprise.

Good for older kids who like solving things. More reading per page, and branching mostly comes in which clues you find.

The Museum Time Slip

Time travel · real dino facts

Your kid touches a glowing fossil at the museum and wakes up in the Cretaceous as a tiny dinosaur. To get home they need three glowing stones, each guarded by a different dino. Fact cards unlock as you meet each species.

Good if you want the player to be the hero (their name in the story) and sneak in some learning.

What the story remembers

  • Brave, Kind and Clever stars. Each choice adds one. The ending gives a badge for your biggest one.
  • Pip's pouch. Sweet berries, a blue raptor feather, warm moss, a croc tooth. Some later choices only appear if you're carrying the right thing.
  • Egg warmth. Warm choices make a giggly baby. A chilly egg hatches with the sneezes (a funny ending, not a bad one).
  • Buddy memory. Your buddy brings up what you did: "You asked a crocodile nicely? You're braver than me."
  • Friends made. The Stego family, the raptors, the crocs. Friends can turn up at the end.

Five happy endings

  1. Home Sweet Nest. Mama Rexie carries Pip home on her head. The valley never rumbles again, because now she tiptoes.
  2. Best Friends Forever. The baby follows Pip home for playdates. (Mostly kind choices.)
  3. The Valley Parade. Everyone you helped shows up to cheer. (Two or more friends made.)
  4. The Big Toot. Pip and the baby sing the lullaby and Mama joins in with a ROAR. (Learned the lullaby at camp.)
  5. Achoo! The chilly egg hatches with a sneeze so big it blows Pip's crest backwards. (Egg got cold.)

How choices should feel

  • Short and pictured. 2–3 choices, each a short action with a little picture so kids who can't read yet can still pick. A read-aloud button on every page.
  • The characters answer first. Every choice gets a quick reaction before the page turns (try clicking the choices in the mockups below).
  • No game over. A "wrong" turn is a funny detour that loops back.
  • Things come back. The berries, the feather and the lullaby all pay off later.
  • Secrets for replay. Hidden choices plus a sticker page showing which of the five endings you've found.

Small animations

  • Everyone blinks and breathes. Tails swish.
  • The egg wobbles a bit more every scene, then cracks start to show.
  • The ground shakes now and then. At the end you find out it was footsteps.
  • Little things move in each scene: smoke, river shimmer, fireflies, leaves.
  • After you pick, the character reacts: a hop, a gasp, a happy wiggle.

Six art styles

Each mockup is drawn and animated live in the browser and shows a different scene from the plan, so you can see the story and the look at the same time. Click a choice to see how a character might answer.

Scene 1 · The Runaway Egg

1. Papercraft

Flat paper cut-outs with white edges standing in a layered diorama. Clouds hang on strings and the sun is on a stick.

Rumble, rumble... BONK! A big speckled egg rolls down the hill and stops right at Pip's toes.

Feels like
A pop-up book on the kitchen table.
Good for
Matches the shelf's kraft-paper home page and Choo-Choo Junction. Cut-outs are easy to animate (wobble, flip, bob).
Watch out
Big dramatic moments, like the T. rex reveal, need extra staging to not look flat.
Effort
Low. Everything can be drawn in code like this.
Scene 4 · Wobbly River

2. Cel-shaded

Bold dark outlines, one flat shadow tone and a rim of light on every shape. Bright, saturated cartoon colour.

Pip... do logs usually blink?

The river is too deep to wade. Three bumpy logs float in a row, just like stepping stones.

Feels like
A Saturday-morning cartoon, or a toon-shaded console adventure.
Good for
Reads clearly on a phone. Faces are very expressive, and action scenes pop.
Watch out
Busy scenes get loud, so backgrounds need softer colours than characters.
Effort
Low to medium.
Scene 9 · Mama Rexie

3. Ink & wash folklore

Fine ink lines, cross-hatched shadows, a muted print palette of teal, ochre and rust, and comic panels with hand-lettered captions. Inspired by Joe Todd-Stanton's Brownstone's Mythical Collection books.

At the top of Smoke Mountain, the rumbling stopped.
It was never the mountain. It was FOOTSTEPS.

A huge T. rex leans down and sniffs. Her nose is as big as Pip! And she is staring right at the egg.

Feels like
A real storybook. The most dramatic and the most "classic" of the six.
Good for
Panels let one scene show a close-up and a wide shot. Great for big moments and quiet ones.
Watch out
The real look comes from lots of hand-drawn detail. Code gets maybe 60% of the way. For the full game I'd suggest hand-drawn or painted scenes, with code only for the small animations.
Effort
High.
Scene 7 · Firefly Camp

4. Watercolour picture book

Soft washes that pool darker at the edges, light pencil lines, paper texture and glowing fireflies.

Night falls. Zippy is snoring on a rock. Then, wiggle, wiggle... the egg starts to move!

Feels like
A bedtime picture book.
Good for
Gentle, cozy scenes and reading aloud at night.
Watch out
Low contrast. Fast or scary scenes feel less punchy.
Effort
Medium.
Scene 4 · Whispering Forest

5. Clay stop-motion

Chunky clay shapes with soft shadows and a thumbprint texture, animated at 12 frames a second with a little jitter, like stop-motion.

Ooh, a shiny egg!

Snap! Rustle! Three feathery raptors pop out of the bushes. “Finders keepers?” asks the biggest one.

Feels like
A claymation short. Very touchable.
Good for
Funny characters and comic timing. The jitter makes even still scenes feel alive.
Watch out
Dark scenes get murky. Needs bright lighting choices.
Effort
Medium.
Scene 2 · Grandpa Longneck

6. Pixel adventure

Chunky pixels, a small fixed palette and a retro dialog box with a blinking arrow.

Grandpa Longneck peers at the egg. “That came from Smoke Mountain. It's a long walk. Who will go with you?”

Feels like
A classic handheld adventure game.
Good for
Crisp, tidy and quick to make lots of scenes in.
Watch out
Less "storybook". Small details and faces are hard to read at low resolution.
Effort
Low.

What I need from you

  1. A story. The Runaway Egg as planned, a tweak to it, or one of the other three ideas.
  2. An art style. One of the six, or a mix (for example, papercraft characters with ink-and-wash panels for the big moments).
  3. Who's reading. Will a grown-up read it aloud, or should kids be able to play alone? That decides how much text goes on each page and whether to add a read-aloud voice.

Then I'll build a playable first chapter (scenes 1–4 with real choices and reactions) so you can try it with the kids before I write the rest.

Mockups are drawn with canvas code, no image files. All characters are original.
